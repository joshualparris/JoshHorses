# Horse-Therapy Discussion Archive — 13 September 2026

This is a consolidated, privacy-conscious summary of the discussion that created this repository. It records the useful content and recommendations without reproducing private health records or third-party contact details.

## Original question

The discussion began with a request for the **best horse-therapy books and podcasts**, especially material relevant to equine-assisted psychotherapy, emotional healing, trauma, attachment, nervous-system work and experiential therapy.

## Initial book shortlist

The first recommended books were:

1. **The Clinical Practice of Equine-Assisted Therapy — Leif Hallberg**  
   Chosen as the strongest broad clinical reference because it integrates research, professional competency, ethics, horse welfare and practical considerations.

2. **How Change Happens in Equine-Assisted Interventions — Noreen W. Esposito & Angela K. Fournier**  
   Chosen as the best book for understanding *why* interaction with horses might lead to psychological change rather than simply asserting that horses are healing.

3. **Equine-Assisted Mental Health for Healing Trauma — Kay Sudekum Trotter & Jennifer N. Baggerly**  
   Recommended for trauma-specific material, including attachment, dissociation, Somatic Experiencing and other trauma approaches.

4. **An Introduction to Equine Assisted Psychotherapy — Meg Kirby**  
   Highlighted as a particularly relevant Australian text with a relational/psychotherapy framework and emphasis on professional ethics and respect for the horse.

5. **Equine-Facilitated Psychotherapy and Learning — Veronica Lac**  
   Recommended for the HERD relational approach, case material, trauma/attachment work, families and ethics.

## Initial podcast shortlist

The first podcast suggestions were:

- **Equine Assisted World with Rupert Isaacson** — a dedicated equine-assisted field podcast.
- **The Journey On Podcast — Jane Faulkner episode** — particularly relevant to Australian equine-assisted psychotherapy, Somatic Experiencing and embodied/present-moment work.
- **Therapy Horse Talk — Sharne Brown** — Australian counselling/equine-assisted perspective.
- **Taking Back the Reins — Dina Shale** — trauma-informed equine-facilitated learning.
- **The Baffling Behavior Show, Episode 51 — Equine-Assisted Trauma Informed Psychotherapy** — an accessible explanation of trauma-informed EAP.

## The question then became more specific

The scenario discussed was **approximately 1.5 hours of horse-based therapy each week**, with a focus on **anxiety, assertiveness and becoming more embodied/present**.

The recommendation therefore shifted away from clinician textbooks and toward material that could make an actual weekly session more useful.

## First one-hour recommendation

The initial one-hour audio recommendation was:

### Equine Assisted World — “Misery Is Optional: Redefining Softness, Structure & Anxiety”

with **Mark Rashid and Crissi McDonald**.

It was recommended because of its focus on:

- anxiety and the nervous system;
- softness versus passivity;
- structure versus force;
- pressure;
- noticing what happens when the horse is not ready or does not immediately respond as hoped;
- remaining clear without becoming controlling.

The central participant question developed from this was:

> Can I notice anxiety in my body, stay present, communicate what I want clearly, and tolerate the horse having its own response?

This was framed as a useful way to practise assertiveness: neither dominating the horse nor disappearing/giving up.

## Main participant framework that emerged

The discussion repeatedly returned to several ideas:

### 1. Notice the body before constructing the explanation

Potential signals to notice included:

- chest;
- stomach/belly;
- shoulders;
- jaw;
- breath;
- hands;
- the urge to retreat, appease, freeze, hurry or control.

The point was not to eliminate those sensations but to notice them soon enough to have a choice about what happens next.

### 2. Assertiveness is not dominance

A useful three-part distinction emerged:

- **Passive/disappearing:** unclear request, excessive hesitation, giving up to avoid discomfort.
- **Controlling/aggressive:** escalating pressure mainly because uncertainty or noncompliance feels intolerable.
- **Assertive:** making a clear request, using proportionate energy, noticing the response, respecting boundaries and adapting without abandoning your own intention.

### 3. The horse has its own response

A horse is not a machine for producing the participant’s desired therapeutic lesson. One of the potentially useful parts of the experience is tolerating another being’s response without immediately taking over, withdrawing or treating it as personal rejection.

### 4. Do not over-interpret horse behaviour

The discussion became more evidence-conscious as it progressed. A horse walking away, approaching, freezing or resisting may be therapeutically interesting because of **the participant’s reaction to it**, but the behaviour should not automatically be interpreted as a diagnosis or accurate mirror of the person’s inner state.

### 5. Transfer matters more than a profound arena moment

A useful session should eventually connect with ordinary life:

- asking clearly;
- saying no;
- noticing activation before reacting;
- tolerating another person’s disappointment;
- staying present through relational tension;
- reducing automatic withdrawal/appeasement;
- avoiding excessive pressure when anxious about an outcome.

## Evidence correction/qualification

Further research for this repository identified a 2025 systematic review of 31 equine-assisted-interaction studies involving PTSD, anxiety and/or mood disorders. The review found positive effects reported across the quantitative studies but also substantial limitations: few strong randomised trials, heterogeneous methods, inconsistent terminology and incomplete methodological reporting.

Therefore the repository now uses the following evidence stance:

> Equine-assisted work has promising evidence and plausible experiential mechanisms, but it should not be described as a universally proven treatment or as possessing mystical diagnostic powers.

See `resources/evidence-and-standards.md` for the research base.

## Current practical recommendation

For a participant wanting maximum value without spending hours studying:

1. Listen to **Recovery Lab #143**.
2. Go into the next session with **one** simple awareness intention.
3. During the session notice **body → emotion → impulse → action**.
4. Afterward write a three-minute debrief.
5. Practise **one** small transfer behaviour in ordinary life that week.
6. Later listen to the Equine Assisted World anxiety/pressure episode and the Jane Faulkner Journey On episode.

The goal is not to become an expert on equine therapy before experiencing it. The goal is enough understanding to become more available to the experience itself.
