## Dubbo

### Arrow Equine Assisted Therapy & Counselling

A public Dubbo service offering equine-assisted psychotherapy/learning and conventional counselling. Its website states that the practitioner is certified through the Equine Psychotherapy Institute and is an Australian Counselling Association member.

Website: https://www.arrowequinetherapy.com.au/

Its publicly described focus areas include emotional awareness/regulation, distress tolerance, confidence, boundaries, relationship skills, stress reduction, attention and connection with nature.

This listing is included as a **local field reference**, not as a recommendation or comparison with any current treating practitioner.

### Turf The Lot

Public website: https://www.turfthelot.com.au/

The publicly indexed website describes Turf The Lot as a long-established Dubbo turf-growing/landscaping business. As of this research date, web searches did **not** locate a public horse-therapy program page on the Turf The Lot website. Accordingly this repository does not make claims about any private or informal equine-therapy arrangement there.

That distinction is deliberate: personal arrangements and private correspondence should not be converted into public claims simply because the venue/business can be found online.

# Australian equine-assisted psychotherapy ecosystem

## Equine and Animal Assisted Psychotherapy Institute (EAAPI / EPI)

Website: https://www.equinepsychotherapy.net.au/

Australian training organisation founded by Meg Kirby. It teaches Equine Assisted Psychotherapy, Equine Assisted Learning and animal-assisted approaches.

PACFA currently lists its postgraduate-equivalent Equine Assisted Psychotherapy program as an accredited advanced specialist program for registered practitioners, with accreditation listed to 27 October 2027:

https://pacfa.org.au/portal/portal/Training-and-Careers/Accreditation/Specialist-Courses.aspx

Practitioner Code of Ethics:
https://www.equinepsychotherapy.net.au/code-of-ethics/

One especially useful ethical distinction in the code is that Equine Assisted Learning practitioners should not present themselves as providing psychotherapy/therapy unless they also hold the appropriate mental-health qualifications.

## Equine Assisted Therapy Australia practitioner directory

Directory: https://equineassistedtherapyaustralia.com.au/practitioners/

Useful for seeing how qualifications vary across Australian providers. The directory itself states that listed practitioners have completed its training but that the organisation does not regulate each practitioner’s standard of practice. That is an important reminder to assess the individual clinician’s underlying qualifications and professional accountability.

## Bendigo Health — Equine Assisted Learning

Bendigo Health Carer Support Services is a useful example of an Australian public health service offering equine-assisted learning as a wellbeing/carer program.

Current events page: https://www.bendigohealth.org.au/css/events/

The September–December 2026 listings describe EAL in Woodend as physically and psychologically safe experiences with horses, with **no riding required** and no prior horse knowledge necessary. The service describes it as an opportunity to explore troubling issues/feelings or simply enjoy being with horses.

This is also notable because an earlier March 2026 Bendigo Health bulletin found in the private research archive advertised an “Equine Assisted Wellbeing” program in Woodend and Mildura.

# What to check when evaluating an Australian provider

Because “equine therapy” is not one uniformly regulated profession in Australia, useful questions include:

1. **What exactly is the service?** Psychotherapy, counselling, equine-assisted learning, horsemanship, riding, occupational therapy, coaching, or something else?
2. **Who is responsible for mental-health treatment?** What is their base qualification and professional registration/membership?
3. **What specific equine-assisted training have they completed?**
4. **Who is responsible for horse handling and horse welfare?**
5. **Is there an intake/assessment and an agreed treatment goal?**
6. **How is progress reviewed?**
7. **What happens if a participant becomes highly distressed?**
8. **How are physical risks, allergies, weather, footwear and emergency procedures managed?**
9. **What is the horse allowed to say “no” to?** Good horse welfare matters ethically and can improve the quality of the interaction.
10. **How are confidentiality and records handled?**

These questions are not a test that one particular model must pass. They help distinguish accountable clinical practice from vague therapeutic marketing.

# Australian professional context

A 2025 Australian paper on equine-assisted mental-health interventions notes that Australia lacks a single formal regulatory body specifically governing all equine-assisted interventions. It argues for clearer professional guidance and attention to ethical incorporation of horses into social-work practice:

https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02650533.2025.2495741

This makes ordinary mental-health professional standards especially important: qualifications, scope, informed consent, privacy, supervision, referral pathways, evidence-informed practice and safe management of both human and equine participants.
