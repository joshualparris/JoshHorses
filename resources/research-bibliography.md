# Research Bibliography

**Last checked:** 13 September 2026

This is a compact evidence index for the JoshHorses resource base. It prioritises systematic reviews, anxiety/PTSD studies, horse-welfare research and Australian professional context. It is not exhaustive.

## Systematic reviews — human outcomes

### Kapteijn C, Reinders N, Hueting M, et al. (2025)

**Are Equine Assisted Interactions (EAI) a Dark Horse Bet? A Systematic Review on Measuring the Psychological and Physiological Effects of EAI on PTSD, Anxiety and/or Mood Disorders.** *Psychiatry and Clinical Psychopharmacology*, 35(Suppl 1), S90-S112.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.5152/pcp.2025.24958
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40865011/
- Free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12410245/
- PMCID: PMC12410245
- PMID: 40865011

**Why it matters:** one of the strongest recent high-level reviews for the exact cluster of PTSD, anxiety and mood disorders. It included 31 eligible studies. Although all quantitative studies reported one or more positive treatment effects, the authors found few randomised controlled studies, missing methodological detail and large heterogeneity in populations, interventions, terminology and measures.

**JoshHorses interpretation:** promising field; not yet strong enough for blanket claims of proven efficacy.

### Li J, Sánchez-García R. (2023)

**Equine-assisted interventions for veterans with posttraumatic stress disorder: a systematic review.** *Frontiers in Psychiatry*, 14, 1277338.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1277338
- Full text: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1277338/full

**Why it matters:** focused review of recent veteran/PTSD studies. Ten studies were included; the review found generally beneficial psychological outcomes but highlighted small samples, inconsistent intervention descriptions and limited follow-up.

**Caution:** results in military-veteran PTSD populations should not automatically be generalised to ordinary anxiety or every form of equine-assisted work.

### Kovács G, van Dijke A, Enders-Slegers M-J. (2020)

**Psychodynamic Based Equine-Assisted Psychotherapy in Adults with Intertwined Personality Problems and Traumatization: A Systematic Review.** *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 17(16), 5661.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17165661
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32764468/
- Free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7460234/
- PMID: 32764468

**Why it matters:** useful review of adult psychotherapy/trauma literature. It found that methodological limitations restricted firm conclusions, while identifying potentially useful experiential features and interpersonal-trust themes.

### Anestis MD, Anestis JC, Zawilinski LL, Hopkins TA, Lilienfeld SO. (2014)

**Equine-related treatments for mental disorders lack empirical support: a systematic review of empirical investigations.** *Journal of Clinical Psychology*, 70(12), 1115-1132.

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24953870/
- PMID: 24953870

**Why it matters:** an important sceptical baseline. At the time, the review found major validity problems and insufficient evidence to support broad clinical marketing claims.

**How to read it now:** it predates much of the newer research. It should not be treated as the final word in 2026, but its methodological criticisms remain relevant and are echoed in newer reviews.

## Anxiety-relevant studies

### Alfonso SV, Alfonso LA, Llabre MM, Fernandez MI. (2015)

**Project Stride: An Equine-Assisted Intervention to Reduce Symptoms of Social Anxiety in Young Women.** *Explore*, 11(6), 461-467.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.explore.2015.08.003
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26386749/
- PMID: 26386749

**Design:** randomised pilot trial.

**Why it matters:** directly addresses social anxiety. The intervention combined equine-assisted activities with cognitive-behavioural strategies over six sessions and reported greater reductions in social-anxiety scores than the no-treatment control.

**Limitation:** only 12 young women; the intervention was multi-component, so the horse-specific contribution cannot be cleanly isolated.

### Earles JL, Vernon LL, Yetz JP. (2015)

**Equine-assisted therapy for anxiety and posttraumatic stress symptoms.** *Journal of Traumatic Stress*, 28(2), 149-152.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.21990
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25782709/
- PMID: 25782709

**Design:** small uncontrolled intervention study; 16 participants, six weekly two-hour sessions.

**Findings:** post-treatment reductions were reported in PTSD symptoms, generalised anxiety and depression, alongside increased mindfulness strategies.

**Limitation:** no randomised control group, small self-selected sample and limited ability to infer causation.

## PTSD / trauma studies worth knowing

### Equine-Assisted Therapy for Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Among Military Veterans: An Open Trial (2021)

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34464523/
- PMID: 34464523

Sixty-three treatment-seeking veterans enrolled. Clinician-rated and self-reported PTSD and depression symptoms fell from pretreatment to post-treatment and improvements persisted at three-month follow-up.

**Why useful:** substantially larger than many earlier EAI studies and uses recognised diagnostic/outcome measures.

**Limitation:** open trial rather than a randomised comparison, so improvements cannot be attributed solely to the equine component with certainty.

### Equine-Assisted Therapy for Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Among First Responders (2022)

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36542680/
- PMID: 36542680

Seven first responders completed an eight-week, 90-minute program. The pilot found preliminary symptom improvements, especially for depressive and trauma-related symptoms, with qualitative reports of reduced anxiety, mindfulness and increased trust.

**Limitation:** extremely small pilot sample.

## Horse welfare

### Baxley BH, Rodriguez-Mori H, Anderson NC. (2024)

**Welfare and stress of horses used for Equine-assisted services: A systematic review.** *Applied Animal Behaviour Science*, 280, 106424.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2024.106424
- Publisher: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159124002727

**Review size:** 28 papers.

Common horse-welfare measures included head/ear/tail posture, heart rate, heart-rate variability and cortisol. The review found inconsistent conclusions, small horse samples and major methodological variability. In 17 of 28 articles there was either no significant stress difference or no firm conclusion.

**Why it matters:** the welfare evidence does not justify either simplistic extreme — “therapy horses are necessarily stressed” or “therapy horses obviously enjoy the work”. Welfare has to be assessed, protected and individualised.

## Australian professional context

### Stacy M, Shabbar F. (2025)

**Healing in relationships, the power of equine-assisted mental health interventions.** *Journal of Social Work Practice*, 2025, pp. 151-167.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/02650533.2025.2495741
- Full article page: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02650533.2025.2495741

The paper discusses equine-assisted intervention in Australian child mental-health/social-work practice, including self-regulation, confidence, self-esteem, empathy and trauma-informed/child-centred approaches. It is also useful for understanding the professional/regulatory gaps around EAI in Australia.

## How to use this bibliography

For a participant wanting to understand weekly therapy rather than conduct academic research:

1. Read the abstract/conclusion of the **2025 Kapteijn et al. review** first.
2. Read **Project Stride** and **Earles et al.** because they are directly relevant to anxiety.
3. Use the **Baxley et al. horse-welfare review** as a reminder that the horse’s experience matters and that welfare claims need evidence too.
4. Keep the **2014 sceptical review** in the folder so the resource base stays intellectually honest.
5. Treat professional-model websites as information about how those organisations practise — not as independent proof that their model is effective.

## Evidence language used throughout JoshHorses

- **“Found/reported”** = a particular study observed something.
- **“Suggests/promising”** = signal worth taking seriously, but uncertainty remains.
- **“Supported by a systematic review”** = stronger synthesis, though only as good as the studies included.
- **“Proven”** = generally avoided for this field because the current evidence base is too heterogeneous for broad universal claims.
