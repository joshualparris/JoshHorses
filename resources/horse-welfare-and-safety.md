# Horse Welfare and Safety in Equine-Assisted Work

A therapy resource base should take the horse’s welfare as seriously as the participant’s experience. A horse is not therapeutic equipment.

## Best international welfare guideline

### IAHAIO International Guidelines on Care, Training and Welfare Requirements for Equines in Equine-Assisted Services

Official page and download:
https://iahaio.org/iahaio-international-guidelines-on-care-training-and-welfare-requirements-for-equines-in-equine-assisted-services/

The guidelines were developed by an international task force and apply across therapy, learning and adaptive/therapeutic horsemanship. Key principles include:

- a formal process for monitoring equine care and welfare;
- understanding equine behaviour, cognition, communication, positive welfare states and signs of stress/pain/fatigue;
- meeting species-appropriate physical and psychological needs;
- appropriately qualified and experienced people being responsible for equine care, training and handling.

## Five Domains welfare model

PATH International overview:
https://pathintl.org/resources/applying-the-five-domains-to-enhance-wellbeing-of-equine-assisted-service-horses/

The Five Domains provide a useful structure for thinking about:

1. **Nutrition** — food, water and body condition.
2. **Physical environment** — space, shelter and suitable surroundings.
3. **Health** — veterinary, dental, hoof and physical fitness needs.
4. **Behavioural interactions** — ability to perform horse behaviours; social contact with horses; quality and amount of human interaction; choice and agency.
5. **Mental state** — the overall affective experience resulting from the other domains.

For therapy work, Domain 4 is especially important: *How many sessions is this horse doing? What does this particular horse enjoy/dislike? Does it have meaningful choices?*

## Evidence on therapy-horse welfare

### Welfare and stress of horses used for Equine-assisted services: A systematic review (2024)

DOI: 10.1016/j.applanim.2024.106424

Publisher page:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159124002727

The review included 28 papers. Horse-welfare studies commonly used behavioural indicators such as head/ear/tail posture and physiological measures such as heart rate, heart-rate variability and cortisol. The review found inconsistent conclusions and called for more robust research.

**Important implication:** it is no more scientifically responsible to assume that a therapy horse is stressed than to assume that therapy horses automatically love the work. Welfare needs to be actively observed and managed.

## Australian horse behaviour/welfare material

### RSPCA Australia — horse behaviour

https://kb.rspca.org.au/categories/sport-entertainment-and-work/recreational-horses/caring-for-your-horse/what-are-the-key-things-i-should-understand-about-horse-behaviour

Useful basics:

- horses can respond strongly to perceived danger;
- a horse that feels unable to escape may kick, strike or bite;
- handlers should read body language and avoid escalating pressure until the horse feels forced into defence;
- movement, social and behavioural needs matter.

### RSPCA Australia — training and welfare

https://www.rspca.org.au/latest-news/blog/a-new-way-of-thinking-horse-training-and-welfare/

This article highlights predictable, consistent training, learning theory, positive reinforcement and recognising behaviours that may indicate stress or conflict.

## Why welfare matters therapeutically too

A model that talks about boundaries, consent, trust and regulation while ignoring the horse’s boundaries creates a contradiction.

Useful questions for a participant to observe—not to police the practitioner with, but to understand the setting—include:

- Does the horse have opportunities to move away?
- Is reluctance automatically treated as “resistance” to be overcome?
- Are signs of possible pain/stress taken seriously?
- Is the horse used in excessive back-to-back sessions?
- Does the facilitator distinguish ordinary horse behaviour from a psychological interpretation about the client?
- Are handling instructions clear and safety-oriented?

## Participant safety basics

Follow the on-site practitioner/equine specialist’s instructions because individual horses and facilities differ. In general:

- wear appropriate closed footwear;
- avoid wrapping lead ropes around hands/body;
- be aware of kick/strike/bite zones and horse movement;
- tell the practitioner about allergies, physical limitations or strong fear responses;
- do not assume a calm-looking horse cannot react suddenly;
- ask before feeding, touching, approaching from unusual angles or entering an enclosure;
- stop and communicate if you feel physically unsafe.

Psychological growth never requires ignoring physical safety.
