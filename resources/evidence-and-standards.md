# Evidence, Standards and Terminology

**Research refresh: 13 September 2026**

This file deliberately includes both supportive and sceptical evidence. Equine-assisted work is a heterogeneous field: studies may investigate psychotherapy, riding, learning, veterans, children, trauma, anxiety or general wellbeing under similar labels. Results should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Bottom line

The fairest current summary is:

- there are **credible positive signals** for some psychological outcomes;
- there are small randomised and non-randomised studies relevant to anxiety and trauma;
- participant experiences can be meaningful and clinically useful;
- but the research base still has **few strong randomised trials, small samples, inconsistent terminology and substantial intervention heterogeneity**;
- therefore strong claims such as “horse therapy is proven to treat anxiety” go beyond the evidence.

It is reasonable to view equine-assisted psychotherapy as a potentially useful **experiential psychotherapy context or adjunct**, provided it is delivered within appropriate professional scope, safety and ethical practice.

## Most useful recent systematic review

### Are Equine Assisted Interactions (EAI) a Dark Horse Bet? — systematic review

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40865011/

Free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12410245/

This 2025 review examined **31 eligible studies** involving PTSD, anxiety and/or mood disorders. All quantitative studies reported one or more positive psychological treatment effects, but the authors emphasised that well-designed studies were sparse, randomised controlled trials were few, and studies varied substantially in populations, interventions, terminology and measures.

**Takeaway:** encouraging, but not a licence for certainty.

## Adult psychotherapy/trauma review

### Psychodynamic Based Equine-Assisted Psychotherapy in Adults with Intertwined Personality Problems and Traumatization — systematic review

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32764468/

Free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7460234/

The 2020 review found methodological limitations across much of the literature. It nevertheless identified potentially useful experiential features and themes of interpersonal trust while calling for better clinical studies.

## PTSD-focused review

### Equine-assisted interventions for veterans with posttraumatic stress disorder — systematic review

Frontiers in Psychiatry (open access): https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1277338/full

Useful for understanding the PTSD literature, intervention variation and methodological limitations. Veteran/PTSD results should not be automatically generalised to every participant or every anxiety presentation.

## Important sceptical review

### Equine-related treatments for mental disorders lack empirical support — systematic review (2014)

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24953870/

This older review concluded that the studies available at that time had major threats to validity and did not justify broad clinical claims. It is important historically because it explains why current resource bases should not repeat therapeutic marketing claims as established fact.

The evidence base has expanded since 2014, but the methodological concerns have not disappeared; the 2025 review still identifies major study-quality problems.

# Anxiety-relevant individual studies

## Project Stride — social anxiety in young women (2015)

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26386749/

A very small randomised pilot trial (12 women aged 18–29) combined equine-assisted activities with cognitive-behavioural strategies over six sessions. The intervention group showed larger reductions in social-anxiety scores than the no-treatment control at post-treatment and six-week follow-up.

**Why interesting:** it directly addresses anxiety and uses a controlled design.  
**Why not definitive:** N=12 is tiny and the intervention combined horse work with CBT, so the horse-specific contribution cannot simply be isolated.

## Equine-Assisted Therapy for Anxiety and Posttraumatic Stress Symptoms (2015)

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25782709/

Six weekly two-hour sessions with 16 participants were associated with reductions in self-reported PTSD symptoms and generalised anxiety and increases in mindfulness strategies.

**Why interesting:** relevant to anxiety, trauma and present-moment awareness.  
**Why limited:** small sample, volunteers, no randomised control group; the authors themselves called for larger randomised studies and longer follow-up.

# Professional models and standards

## EAGALA

Official model overview: https://www.eagala.org/about-us/what-is-the-eagala-model/

Research resources: https://www.eagala.org/equine-therapy-research/

Code of Ethics PDF: https://www.eagala.org/wp-content/uploads/Eagala-Code-of-Ethics_revised__approved_11.7.20.pdf

The EAGALA model is:

- ground-based rather than ridden;
- experiential and client-centred;
- delivered with a credentialed mental-health professional and qualified equine specialist;
- structured so the mental-health professional focuses on psychological process while the equine specialist focuses on horse behaviour/welfare and physical safety;
- governed by an ethics framework covering human and horse welfare, professional scope, confidentiality and safety.

EAGALA is one model, not a synonym for all equine-assisted psychotherapy.

## PATH International

Standards Manual: https://pathintl.org/resources/standards-manual/

Equine Specialist in Mental Health & Learning: https://pathintl.org/certification/mental-health-and-learning/

General resources including EFP/EAL manuals: https://pathintl.org/topics/general/

PATH Intl. develops voluntary standards, certification and accreditation around equine-assisted services. Its standards include safety, precautions/contraindications, ethics, terminology and equine welfare.

PATH’s terminology is especially useful when trying to distinguish psychotherapy, learning and therapeutic/adaptive horsemanship.

# Australian professional context

Australia does not have one statutory regulator specifically governing all equine-assisted interventions. This makes the practitioner’s **underlying professional qualification, registration/membership, scope of practice, insurance, horse competence, safety systems and specific equine-assisted training** important.

A 2025 Australian social-work paper notes the lack of a single formal Australian regulatory body for EAI and discusses the implications for professional practice:

https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02650533.2025.2495741

The Psychotherapy and Counselling Federation of Australia (PACFA) currently lists the Equine and Animal Assisted Psychotherapy Institute’s postgraduate-equivalent Equine Assisted Psychotherapy program as an accredited advanced specialist program for already-registered practitioners, with its listed accreditation running to 27 October 2027:

https://pacfa.org.au/portal/portal/Training-and-Careers/Accreditation/Specialist-Courses.aspx

Equine and Animal Assisted Psychotherapy Institute: https://www.equinepsychotherapy.net.au/

Its practitioner code makes an important scope distinction: practitioners trained only in Equine Assisted Learning should not represent that work as psychotherapy/therapy unless they hold the required mental-health qualifications.

Code: https://www.equinepsychotherapy.net.au/code-of-ethics/

# Claims to treat cautiously

These ideas may be useful metaphors or practitioner observations, but should not be presented as settled science without specific evidence:

- “horses always mirror your emotions”;
- “the horse knows what you are feeling better than you do”;
- “heart rates automatically synchronise and this causes healing”;
- “horses absorb trauma/negative energy”;
- “a horse’s behaviour reveals the truth about a person”;
- “equine therapy works because horses are prey animals and therefore can diagnose human nervous-system states.”

A horse can provide salient **behavioural feedback in a live relationship**, but horse behaviour has many possible causes. Good therapy should remain curious rather than assigning a single psychological meaning to every movement.

# What is on firmer conceptual ground?

The following mechanisms are compatible with broader psychotherapy and learning science, even though the exact horse-specific contribution may still need stronger research:

- experiential learning rather than purely verbal discussion;
- behavioural rehearsal and immediate feedback;
- graded exposure to uncertainty, size, proximity or interpersonal-like pressure;
- mindfulness/present-moment attention;
- interoception and noticing bodily activation;
- practising boundaries and clear requests;
- tolerating noncompliance or unpredictability without escalation;
- relationship-building and repair;
- metaphor and meaning-making;
- movement, outdoor/nature exposure and sensory engagement;
- generalising an in-session experience into ordinary life.

The strongest therapy is not necessarily the session with the most dramatic horse moment. The key question is whether the experience helps produce safe, durable change in the participant’s life.
