# Start Here — Equine Therapy as a Participant

This page is designed for someone spending roughly **1.5 hours per week** in horse-based therapy and wanting enough background knowledge to participate thoughtfully without turning the session into an intellectual exercise.

The particularly relevant ideas are:

- trying hard to *look calm* is not the same as being regulated;
- pressure is information, but excess pressure can create fear or confusion;
- a horse can refuse, hesitate, move away or offer something unexpected;
- the useful question is often what happens **inside you** when the response is not the one you wanted.

## What to know before a session

You do **not** need to become a horse expert to benefit. Let the facilitator manage horse-specific safety and handling instructions. Your useful job is to become a better observer of your own experience.

Before entering, take 20–30 seconds and notice:

- breathing: shallow, held, easy, fast?
- jaw, shoulders, chest, belly and hands;
- urge to hurry, please, retreat, freeze, control or “do it right”;
- what emotion is actually present, even if it is small.

Do not try to fix those things first. Notice them.

## Assertiveness experiment

A useful distinction:

**Passive:** “I don’t want to make anything uncomfortable, so I won’t really ask.”  
**Aggressive/controlling:** “I need this to happen, so I’ll increase pressure until I get compliance.”  
**Assertive:** “I can make a clear request, use appropriate energy, notice the response, respect boundaries, and adapt without disappearing.”

Horses can make this distinction tangible because vague, inconsistent or over-intense communication often produces an immediate interaction to work with.

That does **not** mean every horse behaviour is a psychological message. Horse behaviour also reflects training, environment, learning history, comfort, attention, fear, motivation and ordinary horse preferences.

## One question to carry into the arena

> Can I stay connected to my body, clearly express what I want, tolerate uncertainty or refusal, and remain respectful of both myself and the horse?

## After the session: three minutes only

Write five short lines:

1. **Body:** What did I physically notice most?
2. **Moment:** When did I feel most activated, hesitant or alive?
3. **Action:** What did I actually do at that moment?
4. **Learning:** What did the experience show me about how I relate to pressure, boundaries or uncertainty?
5. **Transfer:** Where is one ordinary-life situation this week where I can practise the same skill?

Avoid turning the journal into a forensic analysis. The goal is to help an embodied experience become usable learning.

## How to judge whether the work is helping

Over several weeks, look for changes in ordinary life rather than whether every horse session feels profound:

- recognising activation earlier;
- recovering more quickly after stress;
- asking directly for reasonable things;
- saying no without either exploding or over-explaining;
- tolerating another person’s disappointment or disagreement;
- less automatic appeasing/withdrawing;
- less urge to control an outcome when anxious;
- greater ability to stay present during relational tension.

These are examples, not a diagnostic checklist. Discuss actual goals and progress with the treating practitioner.
