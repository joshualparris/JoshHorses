# Session Framework — Regulation, Boundaries and Assertiveness

This is a lightweight reflection tool for a participant in equine-assisted work. It is not a therapy protocol and should not override the facilitator’s instructions or horse-safety procedures.

The aim is to make **lived experience** easier to notice and carry into daily life without turning every session into analysis.

## Before — 60 seconds

### 1. Orient

Look around. Notice three ordinary things in the environment. Let your eyes move rather than staring inward trying to “feel calm”.

### 2. Body check

Without changing anything yet:

- Breath: free / shallow / held / fast?
- Jaw: loose / clenched?
- Shoulders: lifted / dropped?
- Chest: open / tight / heavy?
- Belly: soft / braced / fluttery?
- Hands: loose / gripping / restless?
- Feet: can I actually feel the ground?

### 3. One intention

Choose **one**, not five.

Examples:

- notice before I retreat;
- make one clear request;
- practise using enough energy without becoming forceful;
- notice what “no” feels like;
- stay present when I do not know what to do;
- allow the horse to respond rather than trying to manufacture a result.

---

# During — notice, don’t score

## The four-channel check

When something interesting happens, briefly notice:

**BODY** — What sensation appeared first?  
**EMOTION** — Fear, irritation, embarrassment, tenderness, uncertainty, excitement?  
**IMPULSE** — Move closer, back away, freeze, please, control, laugh it off, ask for help?  
**ACTION** — What did I actually do?

You do not need to explain *why* immediately.

## The assertiveness continuum

### Disappearing

- vague request;
- apologising for taking space;
- waiting for the other being to guess;
- giving up immediately when there is resistance;
- abandoning your own intention to keep everything comfortable.

### Forcing

- escalation because the response feels intolerable;
- treating compliance as proof you communicated well;
- losing curiosity about the other being;
- using more pressure mainly to relieve your own anxiety.

### Clear and responsive

- know what you are asking;
- communicate in the way the facilitator has taught;
- give enough time for a response;
- observe rather than pre-decide what the response means;
- adjust appropriately;
- respect physical and relational boundaries;
- remain present even when the outcome is not immediate.

The middle is not “being nice enough”. It is **clarity without domination**.

---

# If anxiety rises

The task is not necessarily to make anxiety disappear.

Try noticing:

1. **Where is it?** Locate the sensation physically.
2. **What wants to happen?** Run, rush, freeze, appease, control?
3. **What is actually happening around me?** Re-orient to the real horse, facilitator and environment rather than only the predicted outcome.
4. **What is one proportionate next action?** A clear request, a pause, more distance, asking the facilitator, or stopping.

If the experience becomes overwhelming, unsafe or physically uncomfortable, tell the facilitator. “Staying with discomfort” is not a reason to ignore safety or consent.

---

# The horse is not a Rorschach test

Avoid automatically translating every horse behaviour into a statement about yourself.

Instead of:

> “The horse walked away because I am emotionally unavailable.”

Try:

> “The horse walked away. I immediately felt rejected and wanted to stop trying. That reaction is interesting.”

The second observation can be therapeutically rich **without pretending we know exactly why the horse moved**.

---

# After — three-minute debrief

Write no more than a sentence for each:

**BODY:** The strongest physical signal I noticed was…  
**MOMENT:** The moment that had the most charge was…  
**IMPULSE:** I wanted to…  
**ACTION:** Instead, I… / I followed the impulse and…  
**MEANING:** What I am wondering about is…  
**TRANSFER:** This week I can practise this when…

## Optional 0–10 tracking

Do this at most once per session; it is for trends, not performance.

- Anxiety before: __/10
- Anxiety after: __/10
- Felt presence/embodiment: __/10
- Ability to make clear requests: __/10
- Ability to tolerate an unexpected response: __/10
- Felt connection/safety: __/10

A “good” session does not require lower anxiety afterwards. Sometimes useful work is activating. Look for changes across weeks and, more importantly, in ordinary life.

---

# Weekly real-life transfer

Choose **one tiny behavioural experiment** outside the horse setting.

Examples:

- make a request without three paragraphs of justification;
- say “No, I can’t do that” and stop there;
- notice body activation before replying to a tense message;
- let someone disagree without immediately fixing the tension;
- ask for clarification rather than guessing what another person wants;
- slow down before increasing pressure because an outcome is not happening quickly.

At the next session ask: **What happened when I tried it?**

That bridge between the arena and ordinary life is more important than collecting dramatic insights.
